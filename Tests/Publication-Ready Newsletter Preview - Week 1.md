# The AI Week: Bigger Models, Bigger Power Bills

*Week 1 | October 5–11, 2026*

**This week, the conversation around AI shifted beyond what the latest models can do. The harder questions were about who can afford to run them, how they'll change work, and what happens when communities push back.**

A new trillion-parameter model arrived. AI-generated mathematical proofs made researchers rethink part of their profession. And while the technology kept advancing, the infrastructure behind it became impossible to ignore.

Google made a multiyear bet on electricity. Denmark decided large data centers shouldn't automatically get first access to the grid. The common thread? Building AI is one challenge. Making it fit into the rest of society is another.

## Headlines

- **[Mistral introduced Large 4](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4/)**, a trillion-parameter model available in preview, with downloadable weights promised later in October.
- **[New research on Danish workers](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai/)** found little measurable impact on average wages or hours from chatbot use so far, even as jobs began changing.
- **[Google secured major long-term power agreements](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/google-signs-36gw-deal-with-constellation-to-secure-power-in-pjm-service-area/)**, while [Denmark moved large data centers toward the back of the electricity connection queue](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/danish-parliament-adopts-emergency-plan-for-electricity-grid-puts-data-centers-at-the-back-of-queue/).

## Technical

### Frontier models: Mistral makes its next move

The competition to build powerful AI models got another contender this week. On October 6, French AI company Mistral launched a preview of **Large 4**, a model with one trillion total parameters. Parameters are the learned values that help a model recognize patterns and generate answers. Large 4 uses a *mixture-of-experts* design, activating only part of the system for a given task rather than all of it at once.

Mistral says the model is competitive in coding, complex workflows, and tasks involving both text and images. Those performance comparisons come from Mistral, so independent testing will matter before anyone declares a winner. For now, the model is available through the company's API, with downloadable model weights promised by the end of October.

The practical takeaway for companies is choice. More capable model providers can mean more options for price, deployment, and data control. But benchmark scores are only a starting point. A model that performs well in a demonstration still has to prove itself on real business tasks.

[Read Mistral's announcement](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4/).

**Also in technical news:** [Anthropic expanded its Cyber Verification Program](https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program), creating different access levels for qualified security professionals using its more capable cybersecurity models. It shows the balancing act developers face: the same tools that can help defenders find software weaknesses may also pose risks if made freely available.

### Hardware: The race for AI is becoming a race for electricity

The biggest AI infrastructure story this week didn't involve a new chip. It involved power.

[Google signed agreements with Constellation Energy](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/google-signs-36gw-deal-with-constellation-to-secure-power-in-pjm-service-area/) covering about **3.6 gigawatts** of electricity in the eastern United States. The arrangements include a 20-year agreement for 890 megawatts of nuclear power and a separate 15-year deal for roughly 2.7 gigawatts from existing generation in the PJM electricity market.

A gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts. For perspective, these are very large long-term power commitments, not 3.6 gigawatts of brand-new electricity arriving overnight.

The deal helps explain a growing reality of AI development. Companies need chips, engineers, and advanced models, but they also need enormous amounts of dependable power. Access to the grid could increasingly determine where the next wave of data centers gets built.

## Jobs: The changes are real, but the wage effects aren't obvious yet

There's plenty of speculation about AI replacing workers. [A study published by Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai/) offered a more measured picture.

Researchers surveyed around 25,000 workers in Denmark across 11 occupations with high exposure to AI and linked those responses to employment records through December 2024. They found no measurable average effect from AI chatbots on earnings or working hours in that data, ruling out changes larger than about 2% in either direction.

That doesn't mean the workplace stood still. Employers were encouraging AI use, offering tools and training, and changing how some tasks were done. In other words, work was being reorganized even without a clear shift in average pay.

It's an important distinction. AI adoption doesn't automatically translate into job losses, bigger paychecks, or instant productivity gains. The real effects may emerge gradually, and Denmark's early experience doesn't settle what will happen in every industry or country.

## Broader social impact: What happens when AI starts doing mathematics?

On October 6, [OpenAI shared mathematical results](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/) produced with an internal AI model. Some proofs were expressed in *Lean*, a system that can check whether each formal logical step is valid.

That might sound like a niche research update, but it touches a much bigger question: what does scientific discovery look like when machines can help generate proofs?

In a [Quanta Magazine essay published the day before](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-ai-the-end-of-math-as-we-know-it-20261005/), mathematicians described a field struggling with that shift. Correctness is crucial, but mathematicians also care about explanation, insight, and why a result matters. A proof can be formally valid without being easy for people to understand.

AI may accelerate research. It may also force universities, journals, and researchers to rethink how they review and credit mathematical work. The next challenge isn't only producing answers. It's making those answers useful and trustworthy.

## China and open weights: Great models still need a business model

Building a competitive AI model is expensive. Turning that technology into a sustainable business can be just as difficult.

An [Epoch AI analysis published October 6](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-do-chinese-ai-companies-make-money/) estimated that six leading Chinese AI companies together brought in roughly one-tenth the AI-related revenue of OpenAI and Anthropic combined. These are estimates assembled from varying disclosures, not directly comparable audited figures.

The researchers described several ways Chinese firms earn money: consumer apps, selling model access, providing customized tools to businesses, licensing, and supporting larger cloud or advertising businesses. Open-weight releases, which allow others to download and operate a model, can increase adoption while making it harder to charge high prices for access.

That tension matters well beyond China. AI companies have to decide how much technology to share, what services people will actually pay for, and whether their long-term business is the model itself or the products built around it.

## Regulation and politics: Who gets first claim on the grid?

Google's power agreements showed how much energy major technology companies want. Denmark's response showed that governments may not always put those requests first.

The [Danish Parliament approved a change to electricity-grid connection priorities](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/danish-parliament-adopts-emergency-plan-for-electricity-grid-puts-data-centers-at-the-back-of-queue/) on October 1, with the policy scheduled to take effect October 12. Rather than treating requests strictly on a first-come-first-served basis, it introduced categories that give critical services greater priority. Very large data centers generally fall toward the back of the queue, although facilities supporting essential services can receive higher priority.

It's an early sign of a difficult tradeoff. Everyone wants the benefits of advanced technology, but the electricity, land, and infrastructure supporting it are limited. As demand rises, deciding who goes first becomes a public-policy question.

## The takeaway

If one theme defined this week, it was that AI's next stage won't be decided by model performance alone. It will also depend on energy access, workplace adaptation, research standards, and whether companies can build businesses that last.

New models make the headlines. The systems around them will determine what comes next.
